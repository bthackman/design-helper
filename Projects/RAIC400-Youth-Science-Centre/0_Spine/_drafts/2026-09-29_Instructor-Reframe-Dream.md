# Instructor reframe: dream and converge (DRAFT)

*Draft, 2026-09-29. Nothing here has been applied to the spine. Section 6 is the checklist of edits for Ben to approve. Client is fictional (RAIC 400 coursework). Sources: `00_Brief.md` v0.6, `01`–`04` spine files, `2_Site/Site-Search.md`, `2_Site/data/scan_2026-09-28_mountains_reach.json`, RAIC 400 Guidelines pp. 9–15, Client Discovery canvas (values wheel and current logo).*

## What the instructor feedback changes, in one paragraph
Cairn stops being a social-services youth centre and becomes a **year-round expedition camp**. The kids are ordinary students from Calgary, the rest of Canada and abroad, not a vulnerable population. That removes most of the hard things in the current brief: the sanctuary suite, counselling, the "welcoming vs. protected" split, crisis-style supervision, after-school care, Children's Services, transit, and the low-income reach test. It also frees the site to leave the city. What stays is the good part: the four pillars, Competence at the centre, a building that rotates cohorts in and out, and the in-the-building / out-in-the-field rhythm.

## What the course still requires (RAIC 400 pp. 9–15)
The reframe has to keep these:
- **Collective habitat with social content.** The course lists "hostels" and "small students' residences" as valid examples (p. 9). A residential expedition school is squarely one of them.
- **Hierarchy of spaces:** single room of maximum privacy → semi-private lounges → large public dining and gathering (p. 9).
- **Three circulation classes:** public/open, semi-public/restricted, private/closed, plus servicing (p. 9).
- **Long-span (unique) vs. short-span (repetitive) program**, rationalized (p. 9).
- **Building systems in outline** (structure, duct runs) integrated with the architecture (p. 9).
- **Interface with the community** (p. 9: "How to make them a part of the larger whole?"). This is the one a rural site puts at risk; see Section 5.
- Useful support: the YOUCAN sample already says the facility should "host exchange programs with other like-minded organizations in Canada and abroad" (p. 10). International visiting students are on-brief.
- Conscious departures to log: YOUCAN's "centrally located" and "close to public transit" (p. 11). The instructor's feedback overrides them; record that in the decision log.

## 1. Dream: three reframed clients

### Concept A: Cairn Expedition School (visiting classes, two-week program)
**Narrative.** Every week a class arrives at the edge of the foothills with suitcases and nervous energy. They spend a week in the building learning to read a map, build a stream-sampling kit in the maker space, test water in the lab, and do day hikes from the door. The next week they go out: five nights on the land with Cairn guides, using what they built. They come back through the trail door on Friday, muddy, show the incoming class what they found, add a stone to the cairn, and head home. The building is the base; the land is the classroom.

- **Who the kids are:** whole school classes and exchange groups, about 24 per cohort, ages 12–16 (grades 7–11), from Calgary, the rest of Canada and abroad, with 2–4 of their own teachers. In summer, individually enrolled campers aged 12–15.
- **Year-round cadence:** Sept–June, two-week program, one week in and one week out; two cohorts leapfrog so one is always in the building and one is always in the field. Summer, 4-week sessions.
- **What makes the building distinctive:** it has **two front doors**, one to the road (arrival) and one to the land (the trail door). The expedition wing (gear store, packing hall, drying room, wash-down) is the hinge between them, and it's a warm, visible room rather than back-of-house. The residential side is simple: two houses of 12 beds.
- **Studio pros:** the cadence is simple and makes a strong spatial idea (in/out, road/trail). It uses the current program almost directly. One cohort in the beds every night keeps the building consistently full. The drying room and wet gear give a real building-systems story.
- **Studio cons:** a visiting class stays only a week in the building, so "habitat" is closer to a hostel than a home. The residential design risk is that it reads as a lodge dormitory.

### Concept B: Cairn Field Semester (a term-long outdoor-science campus)
**Narrative.** Thirty-two students aged 15–17 from across Canada and overseas come for a full 10-week term. They take their core courses here, but science, geography and phys-ed are taught through the landscape: a snowpack study in winter, a river study in spring. Every third week they go out on an expedition. They leave with course credits, a term's worth of field data, and friends from other countries.

- **Who the kids are:** older students (15–17) on a semester away, enrolled individually through their schools or exchange programs.
- **Year-round cadence:** three 10-week terms (fall, winter-spring, summer), with a 2-week in / 1-week out rhythm inside each term.
- **What makes the building distinctive:** it's a small campus. Real student rooms (two-bed), house kitchens, a library/study hall, a full set of classrooms. It's closest to the course's "small students' residence" example.
- **Studio pros:** the deepest collective habitat of the three. Kids live there long enough for rooms, lounges and house life to really matter, which suits the course's privacy hierarchy.
- **Studio cons:** it's a school, so it needs a full academic program (more classrooms, teachers, a library), and the building gets bigger, not simpler. Only older kids. It drifts from the instructor's "camp" framing.

### Concept C: Cairn Field Station (a youth research station at the mountain gateway)
**Narrative.** A small research station for young people. Crews of 12–16 come for a week and join long-running studies the station has kept for years: a weather record, a stream-temperature log, a bird count, a snow survey. Each crew adds its week of data to the record, and the station's entry wall shows the record growing. Fieldwork is day-out, night-in: the study sites are on and around the property.

- **Who the kids are:** school science classes and keen individual students, ages 12–17, domestic and international.
- **Year-round cadence:** one-week stays, one crew at a time, weekly changeover. No overnight expeditions.
- **What makes the building distinctive:** the building is an instrument: a weather mast, an observation deck, a sample archive, a lab as the heart of the plan, and glazing that frames the study sites.
- **Studio pros:** the simplest cadence (one group, one week). A very strong architectural identity. The lab-and-landscape relationship is vivid for a design driver.
- **Studio cons:** it's lab-heavy and residentially thin. It loses the expedition and the camp spirit, and weakens the Outdoors and Mentorship pillars. Beds are full every night but the social life is small.

## 2. Recommendation: Concept A, with one idea borrowed from C

**Pick Concept A, Cairn Expedition School**, because it answers the instructor directly (a year-round camp, domestic and international, stable use), keeps Ben's two-week cadence, reuses most of the current program, and makes the building *simpler*: one population per week, one bed count all year, two doors instead of a security split. From **Concept C**, borrow the **field record**: every cohort adds its data (and its stone) to a long-running record of the site kept in the entry hub. That gives the lab week a purpose, makes the rotation visible (driver W1), and gives Stone Day a second layer.

**Name.** Recommend **Cairn Expedition School**, subtitle "Academics & Outdoors". Keeping "Cairn Youth Centre" also works; flag it as an open question. The name Cairn fits better than before: a cairn is a trail marker.

**Rewritten mission (draft).**
> Cairn brings young people from Calgary, across Canada and around the world to the edge of the Rockies to build real skills, in the lab, the workshop and the wild, alongside adults who lead by example.

**Client narrative (draft).** Cairn is a young not-for-profit expedition school in the foothills west of Calgary, founded by Rocky Pyle, an engineer who found his way at a mountain summer camp as a kid. Cairn's idea is simple: learn it inside, then prove it outside. Visiting classes spend a week in the building preparing (science methods, building their own instruments and gear, navigation, first aid) and a week on the land with Cairn guides. In summer, individual campers come for four weeks. Schools, exchange programs and summer families pay program fees; Rocky's founding gift paid for the building.

**The pillars and the centre.**

| Wheel element | Carries over? | How it reads now |
|---|---|---|
| Centre: **Competence** | Kept as is | Real skills and the confidence to use them. Now proven on the out-week. |
| **Academics & Technology** (the lab) | Kept, sharpened | Field science and data: the lab and classroom week, the field record. Tech moves from a stand-alone computer lab into the lab, the maker space and the map room. |
| **Making** (the workshop) | Kept, sharpened | Build what you take out: sampling kits, sensors, shelters, repairs to your own gear. |
| **The Outdoors** (the wild) | Kept, now year-round | The out-week in every season, not just July–August. |
| **Mentorship** (adults who lead by example) | Kept, widened | Cairn guides and visiting teachers, plus the Friday hand-off: the returning cohort briefs the incoming one. Alumni come back as junior guides (the Waymakers idea). |
| Band: **Build · Belong · Find the way** | Kept | "Belong" now also means belonging across countries: domestic and international students in the same crew. |
| Eight character skills | Kept | "Leadership: marking the path for whoever comes next" is now literal (the Friday hand-off). |

## 3. Programming

### Annual cadence

| Season | Session | Cohort | Days in the building | Days out in the field |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sept–mid-Dec** (~15 wks) | 2 weeks, weekly intake, two cohorts leapfrog | 1 class, up to 24 students + 2–4 teachers | Week 1: 7 nights (Sun arrival → Sun morning departure to the field) | Week 2: 5 nights out (Sun–Thu), back Friday for the day, depart Friday evening |
| **Christmas** (2 wks) | Closed | — | — | — |
| **Jan–mid-June** (~23 wks) | Same as fall | Same | Same | Same. Dec–Mar out-weeks are hut-based (see below) |
| **Late June** (2 wks) | Staff training, building reset | Staff | — | — |
| **Jul–Aug** (8 wks) | Two 4-week sessions | 24 individual campers, 12–15, in 4 crews of 6 | Wk 1: all in. Wk 2: House A in field, House B in. Wk 3: swap. Wk 4: all in, final build, showcase, Stone Day | One 5-night expedition per crew (wk 2 or wk 3) |
| **Late Aug–early Sept** (2 wks) | Reset | — | — | — |

**The weekly clock (Sept–June).**
- **Sunday morning:** the cohort that finished its in-week leaves for the field. The building is cleaned and beds turned over.
- **Sunday afternoon:** the new cohort arrives (coach from Calgary/elsewhere, or airport transfer).
- **Mon–Sat:** in-week. Small groups of 8 rotate between the lab, the maker space and the classroom/map room, with day hikes from the trail door.
- **Friday afternoon:** the field cohort comes back through the trail door: gear wash, drying room, showers, a return meal in the hall, a short showcase to the incoming cohort (the hand-off), a stone on the cairn, then departure that evening.

**Occupancy logic.**
- **The beds only ever hold one cohort.** The field cohort never sleeps in the building (it returns and leaves the same day), so 24 beds serve two cohorts in the program at once. That's the same space-saving idea as the current summer pod rotation, just applied all year.
- **Full every term night.** About 37 cohorts a year × 24 ≈ 880 students, plus about 48 summer campers. The building is full 7 nights a week from September to June, except Christmas. Summer weeks 2–3 run half full by design (one house is out).
- **The only overlap is Friday afternoon**, in day mode: two cohorts eat and gather together. The hall and the dining room handle it; no extra beds are needed.
- **Luggage is a real design item:** the field cohort's suitcases stay behind while the next cohort uses the beds, so there's a luggage store near the houses.
- **Weather and smoke fallback:** if an out-week can't run (wildfire smoke in summer, extreme cold in January), the cohort shortens its trip, uses the on-site camp (tent platforms and a fire circle, site scope), or day-trips from a field hut. It never takes the in-cohort's beds; for a one-night emergency, the gym can take sleeping pads.
- **Winter out-weeks (Dec–Mar):** based at heated huts or a yurt camp off-site, snowshoe and ski touring in non-avalanche terrain, snow science. Those field camps are part of the story but outside this building's design scope. (New open question.)

**Alternatives tested.**
- **Out-first (out week, then in week):** better pedagogy for analysing samples, but jet-lagged international kids go straight into the backcountry, and it needs an arrival night that collides with the other cohort. Rejected.
- **Split in-week (2 days in, 5 out, 6 in):** cohorts overlap in the building 2 days a week, so beds double. Rejected.
- **One rhythm all year (summer also runs the 2-week leapfrog):** the simplest option and always full. The building design is identical (24 beds, 2 houses), so Ben can decide later without redesign. Kept as a live alternative to the 4-week summer.

## 4. Staffing (simplified)
- **Year-round, about 16:** director, program lead, operations and facilities manager (rural water and wastewater systems are part of the job), bookings coordinator, expedition lead, 6 field guides (3 crews of 8, 2 guides each), 2 educators (lab and maker space), 2–3 kitchen staff, and a duty rota of 2 for nights.
- **Visiting teachers** (2–4 per class) stay in the leader suites and are responsible for their own students overnight, as on any school trip. Cairn keeps one awake duty staff at the night station.
- **Summer adds about 6:** 4 live-in crew chaperones and 1–2 extra awake night staff. Peak about 22.
- Everyone except the chaperones and visiting teachers works shifts and goes home. Counsellors, intake/family liaison and youth workers are gone.

## 5. Site implications

**Criteria that die.** M3 transit, M4 "near the kids Cairn serves" (and the reach score on the canvas), and the whole "central vs. transit" debate (OQ 10, OQ 11). The scorecard's "kid-friendly" urban-context criterion also mostly falls away.

**Criteria that carry over.** M1 land (easy on rural land now, so it stops discriminating unless the site is a town lot), M5 flood (sharper: river valleys in the foothills flooded in 2013), and M6 two approaches, which now means road side plus trail side.

**New criteria (draft).**

| # | Criterion | Weight | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| R1 | **Landscape at the door:** a trail from the site into real terrain (forest, river, foothill) | H | The in-week's day hikes and the trail door both depend on it |
| R2 | **Mountain access:** drive time to Kananaskis trailheads, target ≤ 45 min | H | Every out-week starts with a van run |
| R3 | **Coach access:** paved all-season road, room for a 56-seat coach to drop off and turn | H | Every cohort arrives by coach |
| R4 | **Airport arrival:** YYC in about an hour or less | M | International groups |
| R5 | **Servicing:** municipal water and sewer, or feasible well and on-site wastewater | H | Rural land often has neither; drives the mechanical room and the site plan |
| R6 | **Wildfire and flood:** outside the flood fringe; two road routes out; room for FireSmart defensible space | H | Residential kids in the wildland-urban interface |
| R7 | **Community edge:** next to a hamlet or town, so the public side has neighbours (gym, trailhead, community science nights) | M/H | Keeps the course's "interface with the community" |
| R8 | **Land use** allows an institutional or camp use (county or town) | M | Rural zoning varies |

## 7. Logo direction: rough, unique cairn stones

**What's wrong now.** The current mark is five rounded rectangles (`rect` with `rx` = half the height), centred on one axis, shrinking in even steps. That's what reads as a stacking toy. Real cairn stones differ in shape, thickness and grain, sit on flat-ish bedding faces, and balance off-centre.

**Rules for all three directions.**
- Every stone has a different silhouette; no two share a width-to-height ratio.
- Sizes don't step evenly: a big base slab, one thick lumpy stone, then smaller ones out of order.
- The stack drifts off the vertical axis and corrects, but the centre of mass stays over the base.
- Contact faces are flat-ish and slightly tilted (2–6°), so each stone looks like it's resting, not floating.
- Colour: slate `#34424D` stones, moss `#7A9A3A` on the top stone only (current brand). Monochrome: all slate, or all white on slate.
- Separate stones with a **white stroke of 2–3 px drawn on each stone** (`stroke="#fff"`) rather than precise gaps; it hides small overlaps and gives the same visible joint at every size.

### Direction 1: Field-found (recommended)
**Description.** Five stones with soft, irregular outlines, like river and field stones. A wide flat base slab, a thick lumpy second stone, a tilted wedge set to the right, a small rounded stone pushed back left, and a small moss pebble on top. The stack leans right then corrects left. Slightly rounded corners give a hand-made feel without being cartoonish. Monochrome: solid slate with white joints; works as a single-colour stamp or embroidery.

**SVG approach.** Draw each stone as one closed path of 6–9 points joined with quadratic curves (`Q`), keeping the bottom and top edges nearly straight and slightly tilted (the bedding faces) and letting the sides bulge unevenly. For a reproducible set: sample an ellipse at 8–10 angles, vary each radius by 10–25% with a seeded random number, clamp the top and bottom points to a tilted line, then smooth with Catmull-Rom converted to cubic Béziers.

### Direction 2: Split stone (geometric, faceted)
**Description.** Four stones cut like broken shale or quartzite: straight edges, 5–7 corners each, no curves. Each stone has one lighter facet (its top face), which suggests depth and makes it read as rock, not a flat shape. Off-axis, with the third stone tipped. Precise enough for signage cut from steel or sandblasted into concrete. Monochrome: drop the facet fill and draw facet lines as white hairlines.

**SVG approach.** `polygon` elements with hand-placed vertices; no two stones share an edge angle. The bedding faces are one or two nearly horizontal edges. Add a second, lighter polygon inside each stone that shares the top edge and cuts back to a point on each side (the lit face). Snap vertices to whole units so it stays crisp at small sizes.

### Direction 3: Ink cairn (hand-drawn outline)
**Description.** Six stones drawn as a single-weight ink outline, as if sketched in a field notebook (which suits the field record). Rough, wobbly edges; stones of very different sizes; the middle of the stack drifts left and the top swings back. Only the top stone is filled (moss). It's monochrome by nature and works as a line stamp, a gear patch or a trail sign. Weaker at very small sizes than Direction 1.

**SVG approach.** Draw stones as open-feeling closed paths with cubic curves (`C`), `fill="none"`, `stroke-width` 3, round caps and joins. For the rough edge, either trace a real hand sketch, or add seeded jitter (±1–1.5 px) to points spaced every 4–6 px along each outline. Avoid SVG turbulence filters: they don't scale or print predictably.
