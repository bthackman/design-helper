**Working name: "Trailhead Youth Science Centre"** — placeholder, not locked. ## Portrait

**The organization.** Trailhead Youth Science Centre is a small, mission-driven nonprofit that would rather be known for what its kids *build* — robots, field-science projects, a finished wilderness expedition — than for the crisis history that got most of them there. It runs lean: a founding-style Executive Director, a handful of program specialists, and a deliberately un-expanded physical footprint (3 pods, 18 beds) it has chosen to use *harder* — through a genuinely complex annual calendar — rather than grow bigger. It carries a real, structural tension it doesn't fully resolve: the same building has to be a proud, public-facing science centre and a discreet, always-on sanctuary, at the same time, for different people, often on the same day.

**A typical user's day (summer cohort kid, week 2 of 3 onsite).** Wakes in a 6-bed pod, communal breakfast in the shared dining hall with kids from all three currently-onsite pods — the one time all three cohorts overlap. Morning block in the robotics/maker space or the science/engineering lab, working toward something that will get presented at the exhibition space when their group returns from camp. Afternoon: small-group tutoring, mentorship-scale, same three adults all week. Evening: common/lounge time, the closest thing to "just being a kid" in the schedule. Bedtime supervised by their pod's chaperone. In one more week, this same kid moves from that structured rhythm into an unstructured, physically real wilderness week — the whole architectural and programmatic arc exists to make that transition feel earned, not abrupt.

## Deciders-vs-users gaps

- **Funders/Board want countable outcomes; kids need to not feel counted.** Any space that serves donor tours or outcome reporting (exhibition, flex hall) should stay physically and experientially separate from spaces where a kid is being supported, counseled, or is in crisis.
- **Board/funder risk-aversion vs. the camping program's necessary physical risk.** The weather/smoke resilience modes aren't just a scheduling fix — they're the organization's answer to a real governance tension: how do you keep offering a program built on real risk (wilderness independence-building) inside a funding/liability environment that wants risk minimized? The building's resilience infrastructure (basecamp, flex hall) is literally how that contradiction gets managed rather than avoided.
- **School-board/day-camp partners want a predictable, bounded field-trip experience; the mission population needs the building to feel like theirs, not a rented-out venue.** Worth watching as the school-year day-camp program develops — the same rooms serve both audiences at different times.

## Contradictions worth designing around

1. **Public/civic (science centre, day camps, donor-facing) vs. private/protected (habitat, sanctuary, counseling)** — the sharpest, most load-bearing contradiction in the whole brief. Not new (it echoes YOUCAN's own "welcoming vs. secure" tension) but now sharper, because this building genuinely serves a general community audience on top of the mission population, not just a more/less institutional version of one population.
2. **A single front door has to welcome four emotionally distinct arrivals** — celebratory (summer cohort start), routine (day/weekend visitors), homecoming (break-program alumni), and unremarkable-on-purpose (sanctuary intake). One entry sequence, four different jobs.
3. **A building that is 3 pods "harder" via scheduling, not bigger, has to make invisible operational complexity legible architecturally** — a visitor should be able to read, at a glance, that this building runs on a rotation, without needing the calendar explained to them. This is the strongest case yet for the dual-scale-rotation idea (weekly pod rotation, seasonal building rhythm) becoming an actual locked design driver.
